# 22. PageRank

Last week we ranked documents by their words. But on the web, thousands of pages contain the words of a typical query, and many of them are copies, spam or simply unimportant. In 1998, two students at Stanford, Sergey Brin and Larry Page, described a search engine that also looked at the **links** between pages: a page is important if important pages link to it. They called the score **PageRank**, and their search engine became Google.

Today Google uses many other signals as well, but the idea of PageRank is everywhere: in the ranking of scientific papers by their citations, in "who to follow" suggestions of social networks, in recommendations, in the detection of spam and fraud, and even in ranking the functions of a large program by its call graph.

Computing PageRank is a **sparse matrix–vector product**, repeated until the result stops changing. This week we make it fast:

* **Power iteration** with dangling pages, and how fast it converges.
* **Push or pull**: two ways to compute the product, and why pulling needs no atomics in parallel.
* **SIMD** for the update of the ranks, which is limited by the memory bandwidth.
* **The order of the nodes**: what renumbering does to the caches, and to the branch predictor.
* **Personalized PageRank** with *forward push*, which answers a question about one page without touching the whole graph.

The benchmarks use the web graph _web-Google_ of the SNAP collection (875,713 pages and 5.1 million links, released by Google in 2002) if you download it with `tools/download_snap.sh`. Otherwise they use a generated graph of the same size: 875,713 pages and 5.0 million links, in sites of up to 4000 pages, where 80 % of the links stay inside the site. 15.9 % of the pages have no outgoing links, and the most linked page has 3,827 incoming links. In both graphs the pages are numbered in a random order, as if by a hash of their address. All numbers in this chapter are for the generated graph, measured with GCC 16.

## Links as votes

Think of a link from page $$u$$ to page $$v$$ as a vote: $$u$$ says that $$v$$ is worth reading. Every page has a rank $$x[u]$$, and it divides the rank equally among the pages it links to. The rank of a page is what it receives:

$$x[v] = \sum_{u \rightarrow v} \frac{x[u]}{\text{outdeg}(u)}.$$

A vote from an important page is worth more than a vote from an unimportant one, and a page with 100 links gives each of them only 1 % of its rank. The equation defines the ranks in terms of themselves, so we need a way to solve it.

There are two problems. A page with no outgoing links, a **dangling** page, receives rank but gives none away, so rank leaks out of the system. And a group of pages that link only to each other collects rank and never lets it go. The model of the random surfer solves both.

## The random surfer

Imagine a surfer who starts on a random page and repeats the following forever:

* with probability $$d = 0.85$$, click a random link of the current page;
* with probability $$1 - d$$, get bored and jump to a random page of the whole web;
* on a dangling page there is nothing to click, so jump to a random page.

The PageRank of a page is the fraction of time that the surfer spends on it in the long run. The jumps make sure that the surfer can never get stuck in a group of pages, and they give every page at least a small rank. The number $$d$$ is the **damping factor**; Brin and Page used 0.85.

{% include viz.html id="random-surfer" alt="A small web of eight pages. A surfer moves from page to page: mostly along a random link, sometimes by a jump. A bar for every page shows how often the surfer has been there; as the walk goes on, the bars approach the PageRank values, which are shown as marks." %}

In the language of probability, the surfer is a *Markov chain*, and PageRank is its *stationary distribution*. In each step, the probability $$y[v]$$ of being on page $$v$$ comes from three sources: the jump of the bored surfer, the jump from a dangling page, and the links:

$$y[v] = \frac{1 - d}{n} + d \cdot \left( \sum_{u \rightarrow v} \frac{x[u]}{\text{outdeg}(u)} + \frac{D}{n} \right), \qquad D = \sum_{u \text{ dangling}} x[u].$$

The ranks always add up to 1: the first term gives $$1 - d$$ in total, and the links and the dangling pages pass on the remaining $$d$$.

## Power iteration

The simplest way to find the ranks is to apply this formula again and again. Start with $$x[v] = 1/n$$ for every page, compute $$y$$ from $$x$$, and repeat with $$y$$ as the new $$x$$. This is called **power iteration**, because it repeatedly multiplies a vector by the same matrix. We stop when the **change** $$\sum_v \lvert y[v] - x[v] \rvert$$ (the L1 norm of the difference) is below a small $$\varepsilon$$, for example $$10^{-9}$$.

On the generated graph, the top page got a rank of $$6.2 \cdot 10^{-4}$$, 541 times the average $$1/n$$. It was the page with the most incoming links. But of the five pages with the highest rank, only three were among the ten most linked pages: PageRank is not just counting links, because the votes of important pages weigh more.

## How fast does it converge?

The change after each step on the generated graph:

{: .grid-head }
| Step | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 10 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 93 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Change | 0.43 | 0.18 | 0.083 | 0.022 | 0.0020 | $$1.6 \cdot 10^{-4}$$ | $$5.3 \cdot 10^{-6}$$ | $$2.0 \cdot 10^{-7}$$ | $$7.5 \cdot 10^{-9}$$ | $$< 10^{-9}$$ |

After the first steps, the change gets smaller by the same factor every time, and the factor is 0.85: exactly $$d$$. This is not a coincidence. The bored surfer forgets where they were with probability $$1 - d$$ in every step, so any error in the ranks shrinks at least by the factor $$d$$ per step. To reach $$\varepsilon$$, we need about $$\log \varepsilon / \log d$$ steps. With the same graph:

{: .grid-head }
| Damping $$d$$ | 0.5 | 0.85 | 0.95 | 0.99 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Steps to $$\varepsilon = 10^{-9}$$ | 22 | 93 | 289 | over 1000 |

A larger $$d$$ follows the links more closely, but the computation gets much slower. This is one reason for the value 0.85. The number of steps does not depend on the size of the graph, so the cost of PageRank is about 100 times the cost of one step, and one step is what we make fast.

## Push or pull

A step can go through the links in two directions.

* **Push** (scatter): for every page $$u$$, send $$x[u] / \text{outdeg}(u)$$ along its links, adding it to $$y[v]$$. It reads $$x$$ in order and writes $$y$$ all over the place.
* **Pull** (gather): for every page $$v$$, add up what comes in along the links that enter $$v$$. It needs the **reverse graph**, with the incoming links of every page, as in week 19. It reads all over the place and writes $$y$$ in order.

{: .code-title }
The core of a pull step

```cpp
for (Node u = 0; u < n; ++u)  // first, what every page gives to each of its links
    contrib[u] = out_degree[u] ? x[u] / out_degree[u] : 0;

for (Node v = 0; v < n; ++v) {
    double sum{};

    for (Node u : in.neighbours(v))
        sum += contrib[u];
    y[v] = base + d * sum;      // base = (1 - d) / n + d * D / n
}
```

Computing `contrib` first saves a division per link. On one thread, both directions took about the same time on our machine: 20.9 ms for a push step and 20.2 ms for a pull step. The difference shows up with threads.

{% include viz.html id="push-pull" alt="The small web in two copies. In the push step, two threads send rank along their links and both add to the same page at the same time: a data race. In the pull step, each thread computes the rank of its own pages by reading the pages that link to them, and no page is written by two threads." %}

## SIMD: the rank update

After the sums, a pull step does a few things for every page: compute the new rank, add its change to the norm, compute next step's `contrib`, and add up the ranks of the dangling pages for the next step. Doing all of it in one loop reads and writes every array only once:

```cpp
for (std::size_t v = 0; v < n; ++v) {
    y[v] = base + d * sum[v];
    contrib[v] = y[v] * inv_degree[v];     // 1 / outdeg(v), or 0 for a dangling page
    change += std::abs(y[v] - x[v]);
    if (inv_degree[v] == 0)
        dangling += y[v];
}
```

With AVX2, a register holds four `double`s. Two tricks remove the parts that do not look like arithmetic. The absolute value of a `double` is the value with its sign bit cleared, so `_mm256_andnot_pd(_mm256_set1_pd(-0.0), x)` computes four of them. And the `if` becomes a mask: `_mm256_cmp_pd(inv, zero, _CMP_EQ_OQ)` has all ones in the lanes of dangling pages, and an AND with the ranks keeps only those lanes. As in week 20, use two or more registers for the sums, so that the additions do not wait for each other.

On our machine the scalar loop took 2.7 ms and the AVX2 loop 1.45 ms for the 875,713 pages. The loop moves 5 arrays of 7 MB, so the AVX2 version moves 24 GB/s: it is limited by the memory bandwidth, not by the instructions. And the update is only a small part of a step (2.7 of about 20 ms), so by Amdahl's law the whole step gets only a few percent faster. The time goes to the sums.

## Parallel: no atomics needed

With threads, push has a problem: two threads may add to the same `y[v]` at the same time, which is a data race. Atomic additions would fix it, but they are slow, as the histogram of week 17 showed. Pull has no such problem: every `y[v]` is written by exactly one thread, the one that computes it. Only the change and the dangling sum are shared, and an OpenMP `reduction` gives every thread its own copy:

```cpp
#pragma omp parallel for schedule(dynamic, 1024) reduction(+ : change)
for (Node v = 0; v < n; ++v) {
    // the sum over the incoming links of v, as above
}
```

The work of a page is the number of its incoming links, and these follow a power law: a few pages have thousands of incoming links, most have a handful. With random page numbers, every block of pages gets a fair mix. But many programs number the pages by their degree, and then the first blocks get almost all of the work. On the graph sorted by in-degree, `schedule(static)`, which gives every thread one quarter of the pages, was only 2.4 times faster than one thread on 4 cores. With `schedule(dynamic, 1024)`, which hands out blocks of 1024 pages to whichever thread is free, it was 2.9–3.6 times faster.

Another way is to split the pages into ranges with the same number of links: a binary search in `in.offset` finds where each range should end. It needs no scheduler and is the same in every run. On our shared virtual machine, dynamic scheduling was still a little faster, because it also helps when the machine slows down one of the cores for a moment.

## The order of the nodes

The sums of a pull step read `contrib[u]` for the incoming links of every page: 5 million reads all over an array of 7 MB. With random page numbers, almost every read goes to a different cache line. **Reordering** renumbers the pages so that pages that are linked get close numbers, and the reads of one page, and of the next pages, hit the same cache lines.

Web pages of one site link to each other a lot. A **breadth-first search** over the links (in both directions) visits the pages of a site one after another, so it gives them consecutive numbers. Another simple order puts the pages with the most incoming links first. We counted the misses of the reads in a simulated cache of 256 KB:

{: .grid-head }
| Order of the pages | Misses in a 256 KB cache | One step, 1 thread | One step, 4 threads |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| random (as given) | 95.8 % | 18.6–19.8 ms | 5.1 ms |
| breadth-first search | 59.8 % | 16.1–17.2 ms | 4.7 ms |
| by in-degree | 95.6 % | 18.2–19.0 ms | 4.7–4.8 ms |

{% include viz.html id="spy-reorder" alt="The adjacency matrix of a small generated web graph as a picture: a dot in row u and column v for every link. With random numbers the dots are spread everywhere; after a breadth-first search they move into a band around the diagonal, and the number of simulated cache misses drops." %}

The breadth-first order removed 38 % of the misses, but the step got only 8–15 % faster. Why so little? The 7 MB of `contrib` fit in the 260 MB L3 cache of our test machine, and a miss to L3 costs much less than one to main memory. And most of the time goes to the loop itself: when we made every read hit the L1 cache, a step took only about 20 % less time. Most pages have few incoming links, so the inner loop is short, and the CPU guesses wrong at the end of almost every list (week 17).

The order by in-degree did not remove any misses, but it was still a little faster. The reason is the same branch: when pages with the same number of incoming links come one after another, the inner loop runs the same number of times again and again, and the branch predictor learns it. On graphs that do not fit in the caches, with billions of links, the order matters much more, and there are cleverer orders, such as Gorder and Rabbit Order. **Measure before you believe**: a change that removes 38 % of the cache misses may hardly change the time, and a change that removes none may be faster.

## Personalized PageRank

What if the surfer, when bored, always jumps back to **one page** $$s$$, the *seed*? Then the ranks measure how close every page is to $$s$$, through many paths: they are the pages that matter *for* $$s$$. This is **personalized PageRank**. If $$s$$ is a user of a social network, the pages with the highest ranks are good suggestions of whom to follow; Twitter's "who to follow" service worked like this. It is also used for recommendations, for finding communities in graphs, and for detecting fraud.

Power iteration works here too: the jump goes to $$s$$ instead of to a random page (and so does the jump from a dangling page). But every step touches the whole graph, and every user needs their own computation: on our graph, about 1.3 seconds per seed. **Forward push** (Andersen, Chung and Lang, 2006) is a local algorithm. It keeps two values for every page:

* the **estimate** $$p[u]$$, the part of the rank that has been decided;
* the **residual** $$r[u]$$, the rank that has arrived at $$u$$ but has not been passed on yet.

At the start $$r[s] = 1$$ and everything else is 0. Then, as long as some page $$u$$ has a residual of at least $$\varepsilon \cdot \text{outdeg}(u)$$, **push** it: keep the part $$1 - d$$ as estimate, and pass the rest in equal parts along the links of $$u$$ (or back to $$s$$ if $$u$$ is dangling):

```cpp
p[u] += (1 - d) * r[u];
const double share = d * r[u] / g.degree(u);
r[u] = 0;

for (Node v : g.neighbours(u))
    r[v] += share;  // push v later if r[v] got large
```

A queue holds the pages whose residual is large. The rank always adds up to 1 in $$p$$ and $$r$$ together, and the exact answer is the estimate plus the personalized PageRank of the residual. So the error is at most the remaining residual, and the estimate is never too large.

{% include viz.html id="forward-push" alt="The small web with two bars for every page: the estimate and the residual. At first the seed has the whole residual. Each push moves part of a residual into the estimate and passes the rest along the links. Pages near the seed get large estimates; the residuals left behind are all small." %}

With $$\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$$, forward push took 13.5 ms on our graph instead of 1.3 seconds, and it touched only 19,430 of the 875,713 pages. Its answer is not exact: 8.8 % of the rank was still in residuals, spread thinly over many far-away pages. But the pages with the highest ranks are the ones that matter. For five different seeds, the ten pages with the highest estimates were exactly the ten pages with the highest true personalized PageRank, and of the top 100, 99 or 100 were right.

## Code to read

* Sergey Brin and Lawrence Page: _The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine_ (1998). The paper that introduced PageRank and Google.
* The GAP Benchmark Suite by Scott Beamer, Krste Asanović and David Patterson: `src/pr.cc` is a pull-based PageRank with OpenMP, in about a hundred lines. It stores the scores as `float`, which halves the memory traffic.
* Ligra by Julian Shun and Guy Blelloch: a graph framework that switches between push and pull in every step, whichever is cheaper.
* NetworkX, `networkx.pagerank`: a readable Python version that handles dangling pages and personalization.
* Reid Andersen, Fan Chung and Kevin Lang: _Local Graph Partitioning using PageRank Vectors_ (FOCS 2006), the forward push algorithm; Pankaj Gupta et al.: _WTF: The Who to Follow Service at Twitter_ (WWW 2013).
* Hao Wei, Jeffrey Xu Yu, Can Lu and Xuemin Lin: _Speedup Graph Processing by Graph Ordering_ (SIGMOD 2016), the Gorder paper; Junya Arai et al.: _Rabbit Order_ (IPDPS 2016).
